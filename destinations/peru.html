// The country

Why Peru is
unlike anywhere else

Peru is where ancient civilisation is most legible. The Inca Empire stretched 4,300km from Ecuador to Chile, and the infrastructure they built — the trail system, the agricultural terraces, the observatory cities — is still standing. Machu Picchu is the famous one, but it's a fraction of what's there. The Sacred Valley alone has dozens of Inca sites that most visitors never reach.

Lima is one of the most underrated cities in South America. The food scene is extraordinary — Peru has more variety of potato than anywhere on earth, a Pacific coastline that's defined a cuisine, and a Japanese-Peruvian fusion (Nikkei) that's genuinely one of the world's great cooking traditions.

The Amazon Basin covers 60% of the country. The Andes run through the middle. The coast is dry desert. Three completely different ecosystems in one trip — that's Peru's real claim.

// What to expect

Three completely different Perus

🏔️
The Andes — Inca Country

Cusco, the Sacred Valley, Machu Picchu, Ollantaytambo and Rainbow Mountain. This is what most people come for and it delivers completely. Altitude is real — spend 2 days in Cusco (3,400m) before doing anything strenuous. The trek options range from the 4-day Inca Trail (book 6 months ahead) to easier alternatives like Salkantay or Lares.

🌊
The Coast — Lima & Desert

Lima is the best food city in South America. The Miraflores and Barranco districts are safe, walkable and packed with cevicherías, Nikkei restaurants and rooftop bars above the Pacific cliffs. The Nazca Lines are 5 hours south. Huacachina sand dunes (and sandboarding) are 2 hours south of Lima. Most people underestimate this part of Peru.

🌿
The Amazon — Jungle Peru

Iquitos and Puerto Maldonado are the gateways. No roads reach Iquitos — it's accessible only by river or plane, which tells you something about how remote it is. Tambopata National Reserve near Puerto Maldonado has extraordinary biodiversity — pink river dolphins, caimans, macaw clay licks. Usually done as a 3–4 day lodge extension to a Cusco trip.

// Timing your trip

When to go to Peru

Best months
Dry Season
May — October

Clear skies across the Andes. The Inca Trail is open. Machu Picchu views are guaranteed. Nights are cold at altitude — bring layers. June and July are peak season; book Inca Trail permits months ahead.

Good option
Shoulder Season
April & November

Transitional months — some rain, some sun. Machu Picchu is dramatically lush and misty in these months which is a different but genuinely beautiful experience. Fewer crowds, lower prices.

Plan around
Rainy Season
December — March

The Inca Trail closes in February for maintenance. Heavy rain in the Andes and Amazon. Lima's coast is actually driest and warmest. If you're doing Lima + Nazca + Huacachina, this is fine.

Special visit
Inti Raymi
24 June

The Festival of the Sun — a massive Inca celebration in Cusco's Sacsayhuamán fortress. One of the most extraordinary cultural events in South America. Book accommodation in Cusco 6+ months ahead.

// The highlights

Things to do in Peru

The non-negotiables, the hidden gems and the ones most first-timers miss.

01
Machu Picchu at sunrise — first bus up

Take the 5:30am bus from Aguas Calientes to be inside the citadel before the crowds arrive at 9am. Walk the main circuit, then climb Huayna Picchu or Machu Picchu Mountain for the aerial view. Tickets are capacity-controlled — book at machupicchu.gob.pe weeks ahead.

→ Book direct at machupicchu.gob.pe — third-party sites charge a premium
02
Trek the Inca Trail — 4 days

The classic route from km82 to the Sun Gate above Machu Picchu. Camping nights under Andean skies, cloud forest passes at 4,200m and the most dramatic arrival at an archaeological site in the world. Permits for all 500 daily trekkers sell out 6 months ahead — book via a licensed operator.

→ Alternative: Salkantay Trek (5 days, no permit needed, equally dramatic)
03
Sacred Valley — beyond Machu Picchu

Pisac market at dawn, the fortress at Ollantaytambo (still inhabited inside the original Inca structure), Moray's circular agricultural terraces and the Maras salt pans. This is the part of Peru that most people rush past on the way to Machu Picchu — spend at least a day here.

→ Stay overnight in Pisac or Ollantaytambo for the best morning light
04
Ceviche in Lima — eat like a local

Lima's Miraflores district has cevicherías that rank among the world's best restaurants. Central (currently ranked top 5 globally), La Mar and Maido are the benchmark. But the real experience is eating ceviche at a mercado — Surquillo Market is cheap, chaotic and extraordinary. Go for lunch, not dinner.

→ Ceviche is a lunch dish in Peru — most top cevicherías don't open for dinner
05
Rainbow Mountain (Vinicunca) — early start

The coloured stripes of mineral-rich rock at 5,200m above sea level are genuinely extraordinary. The catch: you need to be well acclimatised first (minimum 2 nights in Cusco at 3,400m), and you need to leave at 4am from Cusco to reach the summit before the clouds close in at midday. Worth every alarm.

→ Do not attempt this if you haven't spent at least 48 hours in Cusco first
06
Lake Titicaca and Isla del Sol

The world's highest navigable lake sits between Peru and Bolivia at 3,810m. The floating Uros islands (reed communities on the lake surface) are accessible from Puno. The Bolivian side has Isla del Sol — the Inca creation island — which is extraordinary and far less visited than the Peruvian shore.

→ Combine with an onward journey to La Paz, Bolivia for the full circuit
07
Huacachina — sandboarding the dunes

An oasis village surrounded by 100m sand dunes 5km from the city of Ica. Dune buggy tours at sunset followed by sandboarding down the faces are one of Peru's most unexpected and genuinely brilliant experiences. 3.5 hours south of Lima on the Panamericana — easy overnight from the capital.

→ Book a dune buggy & sandboarding tour here →
08
Amazon lodge — Tambopata or Pacaya-Samiria

The Peruvian Amazon is the most accessible section of the basin. Tambopata National Reserve near Puerto Maldonado (45-min flight from Cusco) has extraordinary biodiversity — macaw clay licks, giant river otters, caimans and pink dolphins. Most lodges operate 3-night all-inclusive packages that include all transport, guides and meals.

→ Go in the dry season (May–Oct) — river levels are lower and wildlife concentrates

// Before you go
Practical info
🏔️
Altitude sickness is real. Cusco sits at 3,400m. Spend at least 2 full days there doing very little before any physical activity. Coca tea helps. Drink water constantly. Diamox is available at Cusco pharmacies.
💧
Never drink tap water. Always bottled throughout Peru — including in Lima. Avoid ice in cheap restaurants outside tourist areas.
🚌
Cruz del Sur bus is the reliable inter-city bus. Lima to Cusco is a 20-hour overnight — most people fly (1.5 hours). Book domestic flights at least 2 weeks ahead.
📱
Uber works well in Lima and Cusco — use it over street taxis. InDrive is the alternative in smaller cities. Never accept taxis that approach you at airports.
💳
ATMs have daily limits — withdraw the maximum each time. Carry cash for rural areas. Most markets and smaller restaurants are cash only.
// Getting it right
Things to know
🎟️
Book the Inca Trail 6 months ahead. 500 permits per day maximum — they're gone the day they release. For Machu Picchu entry, book at machupicchu.gob.pe directly.
🌮
Ceviche is a lunch dish. Go before 2pm — the best places close when the fish is gone. Never eat ceviche for dinner at a cheap restaurant.
🌧️
Rainy season closes the Inca Trail (February). It also makes the Andes incredibly lush and Machu Picchu beautifully misty. Not the disaster most people assume.
🧴
Sun protection at altitude is critical. At 4,000m the UV is extreme — you burn in minutes even on cloudy days. SPF50+ and a hat are non-negotiable above 3,000m.
🦟
Yellow fever vaccine is required for the Amazon region. Malaria prophylaxis recommended for jungle areas. The Andes and Lima require neither.
